Today I came across an exceptionally sharp speech framework called the "five bowls" structure:

1. **Situation** — Start with the big picture. Help the audience see the landscape before diving into anything. Let them know where we are and where we're headed.
2. **Negatives** — Face the problems head-on. Call out the pain points without flinching. Create just enough urgency that people feel: the status quo isn't good enough.
3. **Precedent** — Summarize past approaches and patterns. Draw lessons from experience. This gives your argument weight—you're not just dreaming, you're building on what came before.
4. **Ground level** — Speak plainly. Use down-to-earth language. Move people with simple truths, not jargon and abstractions. Get on their level.
5. **Images** — Tell stories, share examples everyone recognizes. Land on vivid, tangible scenes. Give people something they can picture in their minds.

What makes this structure brilliant is the sequencing. It's not arbitrary—situation gives direction, negatives create urgency, precedent provides backing, ground level closes the distance, and images bring it all back to reality. Five steps, and the audience walks with you from macro to micro, from abstract to concrete, from "why should I care" to "yeah, that's exactly how it is."

A speech isn't reciting a script. A good speech is essentially designing a cognitive path—one that lets people take in what you're saying, and then agree with you. The five bowls lay out that path with total clarity.

I should practice this deliberately. Communication skills work the same as technical skills—get rusty without practice, become muscle memory with enough reps.

## Key actions

- Learned the "five bowls" speech structure and studied its cognitive progression design logic
- Decided to deliberately practice this method in future work to level up communication skills

## Fragments

The five bowls framework isn't just about speeches. Break it down and it's a universal persuasion framework—upward reporting, task delegation, client communication, even writing, all fit this structure. The best methodologies tend to be like this: the uglier and more plain-spoken the name, the easier to remember, and the more practical it turns out to be. Beats the hell out of those over-packaged "communication models" with fancy names.
